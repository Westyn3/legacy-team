On most estates, the house is what a buyer falls for first. On Lake Lanier, the water in front of it decides much of what the property is worth. A deep-water estate has to work in every season, from the first warm weekend to the end of a dry fall, and that is where I spend most of my time before a client makes an offer.

## Why depth comes first

Lanier's level is never fixed. It moves with the seasons and drops further in a drought. A cove with good water in May can turn to mudflat by a dry October, and a shallow cove can lose its usable water entirely in a dry year. A kitchen can be renovated. The depth at the end of the dock cannot.

So I treat the words "deep water" in a listing as a starting point. I want to know how much water sits at the dock at full pool, and how much is left at typical winter levels. If a boat floats in both, the property has earned the label. If it only works when the lake is full, the price should say so.

## Reading the cove

Depth is one measure. The cove itself is another. The north end of the lake near [Dawsonville](/dawsonville/) holds some of the deepest and quietest coves on Lanier, and deep-water estates on the Gainesville and Cumming shores sit at the top of the range, well past $3M. Within any of those areas, two neighboring lots can face very different water. I look at how open the cove is, how the bank falls away below the surface, and what the view looks like with the lake down.

## The dock and the shoreline

I will keep this part brief, because the dock deserves its own conversation. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers manages the shoreline, and the dock permit belongs to the property. I confirm that a permit exists, the dock size it allows, and how the shoreline buffer rules apply. Those rules can decide whether a lot is lakefront in the way a buyer pictures it. Then I check that the dock actually sits where the depth is.

## The slope to the water

My years in construction management taught me to read a grade, and slope is where that training pays for itself. A steep lot can mean an engineered foundation, retaining walls, a long driveway and a long climb down to the dock. If the lot is still vacant, those site costs can reach six figures. On a finished estate, I study how the grade was handled, how water drains toward the lake, and how easily a family can get from the terrace to the shore.

## Wells and septic

Sewer service rarely reaches past city limits in North Georgia, so a lake estate away from town may rely on a septic system, a well or both. I want each one sized for the house as it stands today, with records to prove it. On a vacant lakefront lot, a perc test shows whether the soil can support septic and what size home it can serve, and I write it in as a contingency. A deep-water estate is a house, a dock, a slope and a stretch of water, and each one has to hold up. When they all do, the property works in every month of the year.
