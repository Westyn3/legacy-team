Milton became a city in 2006 with a clear purpose: to keep the pastures, horse farms and wooded two-lane roads of North Fulton. Large-lot zoning across much of the city still does that work. For a buyer, it means something rare inside the metro. You can keep horses on your own land and still reach Avalon, downtown Alpharetta and GA-400 in about fifteen minutes. It also means an acreage property here has to be judged on different terms from a house in a subdivision.

## Where the land is

Most of Milton's working horse farms, gated estates and acreage homesites sit along Birmingham Highway, Freemanville Road and Hopewell Road. Few properties anywhere in the metro area compare with the farms on these roads, and supply is thin. Several quiet enclaves here sell before they reach the open market, so we stay in touch with owners and agents along these roads. At the top, large equestrian holdings can pass $5M. [Milton](/milton/) has many kinds of luxury property, and this is the part of it that is hardest to replace.

## What holds the value

Zoning is the foundation. Large minimum lot sizes keep the surrounding land open, so the pasture you look across is likely to stay a pasture. [Alpharetta](/alpharetta/) next door is denser and cannot offer the same acreage or privacy, though the two cities share a top school cluster.

Beyond zoning, a farm's value sits in how well the land works. Usable, well-drained pasture is worth more than the same acreage on a steep or wet slope. A sound barn is an asset, and a tired one is a project. Privacy from the road and from the neighbors is a large part of what buyers pay for here.

## The barn, the fencing and the pastures

Karen spent years in construction management before she became a Realtor, and on a horse property she reads the outbuildings the way a builder would. We look at the structure and condition of the barn and of every other outbuilding, each of which carries its own permits and upkeep. We walk the fence lines and note what needs repair or replacing. We also try to see the paddocks after a rain, because standing water is easy to miss on a dry afternoon.

A standard home inspection may not cover any of this in full. When a property calls for it, we bring in the right specialists while there is still time in the due diligence period.

## Well, septic and the lay of the land

Much of this acreage relies on a well and a septic system. The question is capacity. Each should be large enough for the house in its current form, along with the guest quarters, the pool and anything else on the property that draws water or sends it away. Ask for the records early, and expect us to read them closely.

Topography matters as much as square footage. The slope of a site affects drainage, where a new outbuilding could go, and what any future building will cost. A level, open parcel and a steep wooded one can carry the same acreage on paper and serve an owner very differently.

## Easements and land-use restrictions

Acreage often comes with paperwork that a quarter-acre lot does not. Easements and conservation restrictions both need review. A restriction can protect the land around you, and it can also limit what you build or how you divide the property later. We read these with you before you commit.
